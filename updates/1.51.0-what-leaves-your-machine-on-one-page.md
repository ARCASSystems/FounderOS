# v1.51.0 - what leaves your machine now fits on one page

The guards were already there: a secret scanner that runs on every commit, a flip that refuses to push your identity to a public repo, an audit that reads every seat's permission grant, a writer that refuses to put a token in a tracked file. What did not exist was the one page where you could see all of it - and see exactly what leaves your machine, through which door, and on whose yes. Now it exists.

## What changed

- **`rules/security-baseline.md` is new - the security picture on one page.** Three things on it:
  - **What leaves the machine, surface by surface.** The model session (files you bring into a session go to Anthropic under your plan terms - the one constant of using a hosted model), cloud sessions, each voice tier with its named recipient AND its local footprint (browser speech service; Google for realtime, with a local bridge on 127.0.0.1:8756 and 8757; ElevenLabs for the paid mouth - the optional tiers open local-only listeners and store their keys in the gitignored `.env`, and the page says so per tier instead of claiming no listener exists anywhere), the scrape helper request by request (robots check, page fetch, up to three retries), in-skill web fetch and search, GitHub operations through the `gh` CLI you authenticated, connectors, and git push. Each row says when it fires and whether it is on by default. Most rows are off until your explicit yes.
  - **Where secrets live, enforced by code.** Secrets go into gitignored files only: the connect helper refuses any target except `.env` and `.mcp.local.json` (both gitignored), and no model key sits on disk by default because your subscription sign-in replaces key files. The commit-time scanner rejects token-shaped strings loudly - and the page frames the whole guard chain honestly as a rejection layer, not proof: hooks can be bypassed and CI re-checks after a push, so "it rejects mistakes early" is the claim, never "a secret can never land".
  - **The five questions to ask before any yes.** What may it touch, what does it never do, what does it cost, what leaves my machine named by name, how do I turn it off. The `hire` passport already prints these for every proposed team member; the page makes them your own checklist for anything that wants in from outside, no matter who built it. It also says which protections are rules the assistant follows and which are mechanical checks - the difference is the informed half of informed choice.

- **The page is linked where the decision actually happens.** The `hire` passport's WHAT LEAVES HOME line points to it, SECURITY.md now separates the everyday picture from vulnerability reporting, and the README's local-first section carries it.

## Why it matters to you

**If you are new to agents and connectors:** this page is the difference between consenting and hoping. You do not need to read a line of code to know what can leave your machine and what cannot.

**If you were already careful:** nothing changed in behaviour - this release adds no new surface and no new capability. It writes down what the code already does, so you can check the claim instead of trusting it. The page itself went through the same outside review as v1.50.1, which corrected its first draft in six places - the version you are reading is the one whose claims survived being checked against the scripts they name.

## Integration protocol

1. **Copy `templates/rules/security-baseline.md` into your `rules/`.** It is verbatim doctrine - nothing in it needs personalizing, because it describes the machine, not you.
2. **Read it once.** Ten minutes. Check any row you doubt against the script it names - every claim on the page points at the code that enforces it.
3. That is all. This release changes no behaviour and adds no surface.

## No Claude Code? The by-hand path

**Write your own one-page list of everything on your machine that can send data out, who receives it, and whether it fires on its own or on your yes.** Email, sync folders, browser extensions, AI tools - all of it. Then adopt the five questions for anything new: what may it touch, what does it never do, what does it cost, what leaves my machine, how do I turn it off. The page is the feature; the discipline of keeping it current is the security.
