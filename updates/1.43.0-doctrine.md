# v1.43.0 - the doctrine pack

Four new rules about how the OS keeps itself usable, plus two empty templates. No new machinery, nothing to run, nothing to keep running.

## What changed

Four files landed in `rules/`. All four are reading, not behavior: nothing about your session start or your daily flow changes because they exist.

- **`rules/os-as-harness.md`** names the four properties that keep an OS maintainable rather than merely documented: doctrine files say why they exist, scripts document their own contract, a repeated failure gets fixed at the engine instead of corrected again, and the OS fits the person running it. It also states the standing guardrail plainly: this is a self-observing OS, not a self-modifying one. Everything proposes. You decide.
- **`rules/context-discipline.md`** is about long sessions. Where tokens go, what should be code instead of a prompt, and the running build log plus the 30% rule that let a session which fills up resume with nothing lost.
- **`rules/hands-resilience.md`** is the four-rung fallback ladder for when a tool you depend on stops working, plus a registry you fill in with your own tools. It ships with placeholder rows and no claims about what works.
- **`rules/digital-employees.md`** is for later, once several recurring jobs run for you. Two faces per job, propose-only by default, honest status, and the rule that a job's charter IS its permission grant rather than prose sitting beside it.

Three surfaces you already use got sharper:

- The **ship gate** runs a deterministic scan before the reading passes. A leftover `[FILL]`, last month's date, or a tool credited as the author is now caught by a grep instead of your attention.
- **Closing a queue item** takes a one-line verdict: ok, needs-work, or failed, plus why. Never self-graded, and skipping is free.
- **Lint** reports doctrine files that never say why they exist, as one advisory line.

## Why it matters to you

**If you have been running the OS for a few months:** the ship gate change is the one that pays immediately. Everything it catches is something that would have been embarrassing in front of a client, and none of it needed your judgment. The queue verdict is the second: those one-line notes are the only honest record of what your OS is actually good at, and later features read them.

**If you installed last week:** read `rules/context-discipline.md` and skip the rest for now. It is the one that helps on your first long build. `rules/digital-employees.md` will make sense the day you have a job running weekly that keeps getting something wrong, and not before.

**If you write for clients:** `rules/banned-words-exceptions.txt` is small and useful. When you decide a word on the banned list is right for one kind of document, record it there and the gate stops raising it. A gate that re-flags settled judgment trains you to ignore the gate.

**You can skip this pack entirely** if you use the OS as a thinking partner and not as a system you maintain. Nothing breaks. These are notes for the version of you that has to fix something in six months.

## Integration protocol

1. **Take the new rules files as they are.** Nothing personalized lives in them yet, so there is no merge to think about. Say yes when the update offers them.
2. **`rules/commit-naming.md` needs a real look.** The update adds one section (push cadence) to a file you may have edited. Read the proposed diff. If you only ever run the OS on one machine, that section does not apply to you and declining it is correct.
3. **The three changed skills.** `ship-deliverable`, `queue`, and `lint` each gained a section. If you have edited any of them, the update proposes the addition around your edits. Read each diff separately. Never accept all three with one yes.
4. **Try the ship gate once, on a real file:**
   ```
   python scripts/deliverable_gate.py all path/to/something-you-sent-recently.html
   ```
   Read what it says. `SKIP` means a text scan cannot judge that one and it goes to the reading pass. Two of its checks stay skipped until you fill in `brand.font` and `brand.document_author` in `os-config.yaml`, which is deliberate: assuming your brand is worse than admitting it does not know.
5. **Leave `templates/culture.md` and `templates/roles/employees.yaml` empty.** They ship empty on purpose. Fill culture in when you have decisions behind you to read principles out of. Fill the employees registry in when a job has recurred three times and you have corrected it twice.
6. **Skip `rules/digital-employees.md` if `modules.employees` is `no`** in your `os-config.yaml`. It costs nothing to have on disk and reading it now would be reading about a problem you do not have.

## No Claude Code? The by-hand path

**Copy one file: `rules/context-discipline.md`.**

Download it from the repo and drop it in your `rules/` folder. It is the one that pays off with no tooling at all, because it is entirely about how you work through a long session: keep a running log at each milestone, stop and bring it current when the window is about 70% full, and never work out again what you already established.

If you want a second, take `rules/hands-resilience.md` and spend ten minutes filling in the registry with the tools you actually depend on. The value is not the file, it is having decided your fallbacks before the day one of them stops working.

Everything else in this pack needs the OS's own tooling to do anything, and honestly none of it is urgent.
